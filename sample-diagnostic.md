# Clarity Sprint Diagnostic

**Sample document. Fictional client. All names, people, and figures are sample data created to show the format.**

Prepared for **Alder & Quill Studio** (fictional), a 12-person brand and exhibit design studio. Sprint days 1 to 10. Readout. Prepared by Lēdor Labs.

## How to read this sample

This document shows the shape and evidence standard of a real Clarity Sprint deliverable. The client, the people, and every number in it are invented sample data. In a live engagement, every claim below would point to a named interview excerpt, a document the client provided, or a second source that agrees. Nothing here is a finding about a real organization.

The rule the real version is graded against: **no evidence, no claim.** Scores sort the leads. They do not make the decision. A person does.

## Intake summary (sample)

### The decision this Sprint informs

Alder & Quill grew from 7 to 12 people in eighteen months and took on a second anchor client. Project delivery now runs late in ways the partners feel but cannot locate. The sponsor question: *where does project time actually go, and what should change first in the next 90 days?*

### Scope agreed with the sponsor

- One program in scope: client project delivery, intake to final invoice.
- Creative direction and client strategy are out of scope by design. They are judgment work, and the Sprint maps work, not taste.
- A task can be out of scope for change and still appear in the scored map, when the Sprint classifies it to protect it rather than to alter it. Creative direction appears in the map for that reason.
- Findings report tasks and workflows, never people. No individual is scored.

## Evidence base (sample)

- **5 stakeholder interviews** led by Lēdor: both partners, studio manager, lead designer, client services lead.
- **6 employee workflow interviews:** two designers, producer, studio coordinator, bookkeeper (contract), production artist.
- **Document inventory:** 14 items, including the project tracker, the intake form, three recent status decks, and the shared drive's folder map.
- **Contradictions logged:** 3, each carried into the findings below rather than averaged away.

## Findings (sample)

### 1. Status reporting eats a senior day every week

Every Monday, the producer rebuilds two client status decks by hand from the project tracker, a shared spreadsheet, and email threads. Reported time: 4 to 5 hours weekly, done by the studio's most experienced producer because that is the one person who knows where each number lives.

*Evidence: producer workflow interview; two status decks compared line by line against the tracker (sample items E-07, E-08, E-19). Contradiction logged: the partners put late delivery on status reporting; the producer puts it on briefs that do not exist at kickoff. Carried here, not resolved by the Sprint.*

### 2. Projects start before the brief exists

Four of the last six projects (sample count) began design work from email threads because the intake form asks for assets nobody has at kickoff. The missing information arrives later as rework: an average of two extra review rounds per project in the sample period.

*Evidence: intake form vs. six project folders; lead designer and client services lead interviews disagree on cause, agree on the count (E-11, E-14, E-22). Contradiction logged, not resolved by the Sprint.*

### 3. Invoicing is accurate and slow for one mechanical reason

Timesheets are complete but land in three formats. The bookkeeper reconciles them by hand before every invoice run, about 3 hours twice a month (sample figure), with a near-perfect accuracy record. The work is mechanical; the care is real and would move upstream.

*Evidence: bookkeeper workflow interview; invoice run walkthrough (E-25, E-27).*

### 4. The partners' calendar is the hidden queue

Creative reviews wait an average of 2.3 days (sample estimate) for a partner slot, and projects quietly reorder themselves around partner availability rather than client deadlines. Nobody owns this queue, so nobody sees it whole.

*Evidence: studio coordinator interview; calendar sample for two weeks in the sample period (E-16, E-29). Marked as an estimate, not a measurement. Contradiction logged: the partners describe reviews as waiting on client feedback; the coordinator's calendar sample shows the wait is partner slots. Carried here, not resolved by the Sprint.*

## Scored opportunity map (sample)

Each factor is scored 0 to 3. **Priority index = Repetition + Information readiness + Worth - Effective Risk - Exception load, where Effective Risk = Impact - Catch, never below zero.** Impact scores what is harmed if the work goes wrong; Catch scores whether a named checkpoint stops it before it lands. They are scored separately because a single risk number hides which one is doing the work. Three overrides apply: Impact 3 moves a candidate to Keep human at any index; Impact 2 or higher with Catch 1 or lower cannot be classed Automate or Assist first until a real checkpoint exists; and low information readiness moves a candidate to Fix information first at any index. Classes derive in a fixed order. The overrides fire first. When Impact 3 and Information readiness of 1 or lower collide, Keep human governs: the fix is protecting the work, not readying its inputs. Then the bands apply: an index of 7 or higher with Information readiness 3 classes Automate; an index of 4 or higher classes Assist first; an index of 2 or lower classes Do not automate. Every score below carries its reason; a score without a reason does not ship.

### Why these scores (sample reasoning)

**Weekly client status decks.** Repetition 3: weekly, two clients, every week of the sample period. Info 2: source data exists but sits in three places in inconsistent shapes. Worth 3: returns 4 to 5 senior hours weekly. Impact 2: a wrong number reaching a client is material trust harm. Catch 3: the producer approves every deck before anything sends, so a named checkpoint stands in front of the harm. Effective Risk 0. Exception 1: one client wants a narrative note the data cannot write.

**Timesheet reconciliation for invoicing.** Repetition 3: every invoice run, all projects. Info 3: timesheets are complete, owned by the bookkeeper, and already digital; the three formats are the only defect. Worth 2: about 6 hours monthly plus faster invoices. Impact 2: a wrong total becomes a wrong invoice, real money out the door. Catch 2: errors surface in the reconciliation totals before invoices send, a checkpoint that usually catches them. Effective Risk 0. Exception 1: one contractor's format needs a standing conversion rule.

**Project intake and kickoff brief assembly.** Repetition 2: every project kickoff, a few times a month. Worth 3: two avoided review rounds per project. But Info 1 triggers the override: the brief fails because the inputs do not exist at kickoff, scattered across client emails and calls. Automating assembly today would produce faster incomplete briefs. Fix the intake contract first, then revisit. Impact 1: an incomplete brief costs review rounds, harm contained inside the studio. Catch 1: gaps surface at review, after the cost, when someone spots them. Effective Risk 0. Exception 2: the two anchor clients' kickoffs follow their own contracts, so one form never fits all.

**Review scheduling and asset version wrangling.** Repetition 3: daily across active projects. Info 2: calendars and files are findable; version naming is inconsistent by person. Worth 2: removes the coordinator's chase work and shortens the partner queue. Impact 1: a wrong version or a missed slot is contained and fixable. Catch 3: a person confirms each move before it happens. Effective Risk 0. Exception 2: rush client changes break any fixed pattern, so a person confirms each move.

**Print and vendor quote comparison.** Repetition 2: every job with print or vendor spend. Info 2: quotes are complete but each arrives in the vendor's own format, never side by side. Worth 2: quotes differ in paper, timing, and terms. Impact 2: signing on bad terms is material money. Catch 2: a person reads the fine print before anything is signed, so a tool can lay the quotes side by side while the judgment stays human. Effective Risk 0. Exception 2: rush jobs and favored vendors are relationship calls, not data calls.

**Reading client feedback and setting creative direction.** Repetition 2: feedback lands on every project, every week. Info 1: the inputs are clients' words and partner judgment; there is no dataset to ready. Worth 3: this is the work clients buy; hours protected here are the product. Impact 3 triggers the Keep human override: this is the studio's judgment and its client trust, the work clients actually buy. Catch 0: no checkpoint can catch a wrong creative judgment before the client feels it. Effective Risk 3. Exception 3: the exceptions are the work. The Sprint's recommendation is to protect this time by removing the mechanical work around it, not to assist the judgment itself.

**Repurposing project work into studio social posts.** Repetition 1 and Worth 1: a few posts a month, small time at stake. Setup and review would cost more than the work saves, and client-permission checks make each post a small project of its own. Revisit if volume triples. Info 2: the assets exist in project folders; their rights status is recorded nowhere. Impact 2: posting client work without permission is a trust harm. Catch 1: permission rests on someone remembering to ask. Effective Risk 1. Exception 1: most posts follow one pattern once permission exists; the permission problem is the Catch, not an exception pattern.

*Evidence: full scoring rubric and evidence records ship as the Sprint appendix. Sample appendix omitted here; the format is the point of this document.*

## Sequenced recommendations (sample)

### Days 1 to 30: stop the bleeding

- One timesheet format, studio-wide, with the conversion rule written down. Owner: studio manager.
- Status deck data pulled from one tracker view; producer approves before send. Owner: producer.
- Name the review queue: one shared view of what waits on a partner. Owner: studio coordinator.

### Days 31 to 60: fix the inputs

- Automate the timesheet reconciliation the map classed Automate, now that one format is in place, or record in the next report why not. Owner: studio manager.
- Rewrite the intake contract: the five things that must exist before design starts, agreed with both anchor clients. Owner: client services lead.
- Version naming rule for assets, one page, no exceptions list longer than the rule. Owner: lead designer.
- Quote comparison template adopted for every job with print or vendor spend. Owner: producer.

### Days 61 to 90: prove and decide

- Measure the two review rounds the intake fix was meant to remove. If they are still there, the fix was paperwork, not change.
- Decide, with numbers in hand, whether the status and scheduling assists earn a permanent tool or stay as habits.
- Partners review the protected-time result: did creative direction get its hours back?

What this Sprint deliberately does not recommend: a new project platform, a headcount change, or any tool purchase before the Day 61 to 90 measurements exist. The map says the information and the habits are the bottleneck. Tools come after that is fixed, if it still needs fixing.
